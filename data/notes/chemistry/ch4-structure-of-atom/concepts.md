# Structure of the Atom — Detailed Concepts

## 1. Charged Particles in Matter

- **Atom** is made up of smaller particles called **sub-atomic particles**
  - These particles carry electric charge
    - **Electron**: negatively charged particle
    - **Proton**: positively charged particle
    - **Neutron**: particle with no charge (neutral)

- **Electron** ($e^-$) was discovered by **J.J. Thomson** in 1897
  - Charge on electron = $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C
  - Mass of electron = $9.1 \times 10^{-31}$ kg
    - It is negligible compared to mass of proton or neutron
      - That's why electrons are called negligible mass particles

- **Proton** ($p^+$) was discovered during electrolysis experiments
  - Charge on proton = $+1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C (same magnitude as electron, opposite sign)
  - Mass of proton = $1.673 \times 10^{-27}$ kg
    - Approximately 1836 times the mass of an electron

- **Neutron** ($n^0$) was discovered by **James Chadwick** in 1932
  - Charge on neutron = zero (neutral)
  - Mass of neutron = $1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ kg
    - Slightly heavier than proton

- **Nucleus** is the central part of atom
  - Contains protons and neutrons (together called **nucleons**)
    - Accounts for almost entire mass of atom
      - But occupies very small volume compared to atom

- **In a neutral atom**:
  - Number of protons = Number of electrons
    - Positive charge = Negative charge
      - Therefore, atom is electrically neutral

## 2. Thomson's Model of Atom (1904)

- **J.J. Thomson** proposed the first model of atom
  - Also called the **"Plum Pudding Model"** or **"Watermelon Model"**

- **Model description:**
  - Atom is a **positively charged sphere**
    - Electrons are **embedded** in this sphere
      - Like seeds in a watermelon
        - Or like plums in a pudding

- **Key features:**
  - The size of atom is about $10^{-10}$ m
    - Positive charge is uniformly distributed
      - Electrons are distributed uniformly throughout the sphere
        - The total positive charge = total negative charge

- **Limitations of Thomson's Model:**
  - Could not explain results of **Rutherford's alpha particle scattering experiment**
    - Did not describe how electrons are arranged in the nucleus
      - Could not explain the line spectra of atoms

## 3. Rutherford's Model of Atom (1911)

- **Ernest Rutherford** performed the famous **alpha particle scattering experiment**
  - Alpha particles ($\alpha$) are positively charged particles (helium nuclei)

- **Experimental setup:**
  - A beam of alpha particles was directed at a **thin gold foil**
    - A circular fluorescent screen was placed around the foil
      - Deflection of alpha particles was observed on the screen

- **Observations:**
  - **Most alpha particles** passed straight through the gold foil
    - This showed that atom is **mostly empty space**
  - **Some alpha particles** were deflected by **small angles**
    - This showed that positive charge is concentrated in a small region
  - **Very few alpha particles** bounced back (deflected by 180°)
    - This showed that positive charge is concentrated in a very small, dense region called the **nucleus**

- **Rutherford's conclusions:**
  - Most of the mass and entire positive charge of atom is concentrated in a **nucleus**
    - Nucleus is at the **centre** of atom
      - Electrons revolve around the nucleus in **circular orbits**
        - The size of nucleus is about $10^{-15}$ m (much smaller than atom)

- **Nuclear Model of Atom:**
  - Atom has a **dense, positively charged nucleus**
    - Electrons revolve around the nucleus at large distances
      - Like planets revolving around the sun

- **Drawbacks of Rutherford's Model:**
  - **Stability problem**: A charged particle in circular motion should lose energy continuously (according to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory)
    - Electrons should spiral into the nucleus and atom should collapse
      - But atoms are stable — this was not explained
  - **Line spectra**: Could not explain why atoms emit light at specific wavelengths only

## 4. Bohr's Model of Atom (1913)

- **Niels Bohr** modified Rutherford's model to overcome its drawbacks
  - Proposed that electrons revolve in **fixed circular orbits** (shells) at specific distances from nucleus

- **Key postulates:**
  - Electrons revolve in **circular orbits** called **shells** or **energy levels**
    - Each orbit has a **fixed energy** and **fixed radius**
      - Electrons in these orbits do **not radiate energy**
        - This solved the **stability problem**
  - Each orbit is assigned a number: **K, L, M, N, ...** or **n = 1, 2, 3, 4, ...**
    - Energy increases as we move away from nucleus: K < L < M < N
  - Electrons can **jump** between orbits by absorbing or emitting energy
    - When electron absorbs energy → jumps to **higher orbit** (excited state)
      - When electron falls back → emits energy as **light** (emits radiation)
  - The **angular momentum** of electron in an orbit is **quantized**:
    - $mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}$
      - where $n$ = orbit number, $h$ = Planck's constant, $m$ = mass, $v$ = velocity, $r$ = radius

- **Advantages of Bohr's Model:**
  - Explains **stability** of atom
  - Explains **line spectra** of hydrogen atom
  - Introduces concept of **discrete energy levels**

- **Limitations:**
  - Works well only for **hydrogen atom**
    - Cannot explain spectra of multi-electron atoms
      - Does not explain fine details of atomic spectra

## 5. Electron Distribution in Different Orbits

- **Maximum number of electrons** that can be accommodated in a shell is given by:
  - $2n^2$
    - where $n$ = orbit number (shell number)

- **Shell capacity:**

| Shell | n | Max Electrons ($2n^2$) |
|-------|---|----------------------|
| K | 1 | $2(1)^2 = 2$ |
| L | 2 | $2(2)^2 = 8$ |
| M | 3 | $2(3)^2 = 18$ |
| N | 4 | $2(4)^2 = 32$ |

- **Rules for filling electrons:**
  - Electrons fill **inner shells first**, then outer shells
    - Outermost shell can hold maximum **8 electrons**
      - Second shell can hold maximum **8 electrons** (before filling third)
        - This is the **octet rule**

- **Electronic Configuration** is the arrangement of electrons in different shells

- **Examples:**

| Element | Atomic No. | Electronic Configuration |
|---------|-----------|------------------------|
| Hydrogen | 1 | K=1 |
| Helium | 2 | K=2 |
| Lithium | 3 | K=2, L=1 |
| Carbon | 6 | K=2, L=4 |
| Neon | 10 | K=2, L=8 |
| Sodium | 11 | K=2, L=8, M=1 |
| Argon | 18 | K=2, L=8, M=8 |
| Calcium | 20 | K=2, L=8, M=8, N=2 |

## 6. Valency

- **Valency** is the **combining capacity** of an atom
  - It is determined by the number of **electrons in the outermost shell** (valence electrons)

- **How valency works:**
  - If outermost shell has **8 electrons** → atom is **stable** → valency = **0** (noble gases)
    - If outermost shell has **less than 4 electrons** → atom tends to **lose electrons**
      - Valency = number of electrons to be lost
        - Example: Sodium (2, 8, 1) → loses 1 electron → valency = 1
  - If outermost shell has **more than 4 electrons** → atom tends to **gain electrons**
    - Valency = 8 − number of electrons in outermost shell
      - Example: Chlorine (2, 8, 7) → needs 1 electron → valency = 1
  - If outermost shell has **exactly 4 electrons** → atom **shares electrons**
    - Valency = 4
      - Example: Carbon (2, 4) → valency = 4

## 7. Atomic Number and Mass Number

- **Atomic Number (Z):**
  - The number of **protons** present in the nucleus of an atom
    - $Z = p^+ = e^-$ (in a neutral atom)
      - It uniquely identifies an element

- **Mass Number (A):**
  - The sum of the number of **protons** and **neutrons** present in the nucleus
    - $A = p^+ + n^0$
      - Also called **nucleon number**

- **Number of neutrons:**
  - $n = A - Z$
    - Example: Carbon-12: $Z = 6$, $A = 12$ → $n = 12 - 6 = 6$

- **Symbolic representation:**
  - An element is represented as: ${}_{Z}^{A}X$
    - where $X$ = element symbol, $A$ = mass number, $Z$ = atomic number
      - Example: ${}_{6}^{12}C$, ${}_{8}^{16}O$, ${}_{11}^{23}Na$

## 8. Isotopes and Isobars

- **Isotopes:**
  - Atoms of the **same element** having the **same atomic number** but **different mass numbers**
    - They have **same number of protons** but **different number of neutrons**

- **Examples of isotopes:**

| Isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Mass Number |
|---------|--------|---------|----------|-------------|
| Protium | ${}_{1}^{1}H$ | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Deuterium | ${}_{1}^{2}H$ | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Tritium | ${}_{1}^{3}H$ | 1 | 2 | 3 |

| Isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Mass Number |
|---------|--------|---------|----------|-------------|
| Carbon-12 | ${}_{6}^{12}C$ | 6 | 6 | 12 |
| Carbon-13 | ${}_{6}^{13}C$ | 6 | 7 | 13 |
| Carbon-14 | ${}_{6}^{14}C$ | 6 | 8 | 14 |

- **Properties of isotopes:**
  - Have **same chemical properties** (because same number of electrons)
    - Have **different physical properties** (because different mass)
      - Used in nuclear reactors, dating archaeological finds, medicine

- **Isobars:**
  - Atoms of **different elements** having the **same mass number** but **different atomic numbers**
    - They belong to **different elements** but have same total nucleons

- **Examples of isobars:**

| Element | Symbol | Z | A | n |
|---------|--------|---|---|---|
| Argon | ${}_{18}^{40}Ar$ | 18 | 40 | 22 |
| Potassium | ${}_{19}^{40}K$ | 19 | 40 | 21 |
| Calcium | ${}_{20}^{40}Ca$ | 20 | 40 | 20 |

- **Properties of isobars:**
  - Have **different chemical properties** (because different elements)
    - Have **same mass number** (because total nucleons are same)

## Summary Comparison: Isotopes vs Isobars

| Feature | Isotopes | Isobars |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Definition | Same Z, different A | Same A, different Z |
| Elements | Same element | Different elements |
| Protons | Same | Different |
| Neutrons | Different | Different |
| Mass Number | Different | Same |
| Chemical Properties | Same | Different |
| Physical Properties | Different | May be same |
| Example | ${}_{1}^{1}H$, ${}_{1}^{2}H$, ${}_{1}^{3}H$ | ${}_{18}^{40}Ar$, ${}_{19}^{40}K$, ${}_{20}^{40}Ca$ |
