# Structure of the Atom — Textbook Notes

## 4.1 Charged Particles in Matter

- Dalton's atomic theory said atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction
- This theory was proved wrong when scientists discovered smaller particles inside the atom
- The three sub-atomic particles of an atom are electron, proton, and neutron

- Electron was discovered by J.J. Thomson in 1897
- It carries a negative charge of magnitude 1.6 x 10^-19 C
- Its mass is negligible, approximately 1/1836 of the mass of a hydrogen atom
- Electrons are also called beta particles

- Protons were discovered during the study of electrical discharge through gases
- A positively charged particle called canal rays was observed by E. Goldstein in 1886
- The positively charged particles in canal rays are actually protons
- A proton carries a positive charge equal in magnitude to the negative charge on an electron
- The mass of a proton is approximately equal to the mass of a hydrogen atom
- The mass of a proton is 1.67 x 10^-27 kg, which is 1836 times the mass of an electron

- Neutrons were discovered by James Chadwick in 1932
- A neutron has no charge, it is neutral
- Its mass is approximately equal to the mass of a proton
- The mass of a neutron is 1.675 x 10^-27 kg

- An atom is electrically neutral because the number of protons and electrons in an atom are equal
- The mass of an atom is almost entirely contributed by the protons and neutrons since electrons have negligible mass
- Protons and neutrons are collectively called nucleons because they are present in the nucleus

## 4.2 Structure of an Atom

- Atom has a central nucleus where protons and neutrons are present
- Electrons revolve around the nucleus in well-defined circular paths called orbits or shells
- The three subatomic particles differ in their mass and charge

- An atom is electrically neutral because it contains equal number of positive and negative charges
- The atomic model has evolved through contributions from scientists like Thomson, Rutherford, and Bohr

## 4.3 Thomson's Model of Atom

- J.J. Thomson proposed the model of atom in 1898
- It is also called the plum pudding model or watermelon model or raisin pudding model

- According to Thomson, an atom consists of a positively charged sphere and the electrons are embedded in it
- The negative and positive charges are equal in magnitude, so the atom as a whole is electrically neutral
- The mass of the atom is assumed to be uniformly distributed over the atom
- The radius of the atom is about 10^-10 m

- Thomson's model could not explain the results of the scattering experiment carried out by Rutherford
- It did not tell us how the protons and electrons are arranged in an atom
- It could not explain the line spectra of atoms

## 4.4 Rutherford's Model of Atom

- Rutherford carried out the alpha particle scattering experiment in 1911
- He directed a beam of alpha particles at a thin gold foil
- Alpha particles are positively charged particles, actually helium nuclei

- Observations of the experiment:
- Most of the alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil without any deflection
- Some of the alpha particles were deflected by small angles
- A few alpha particles were deflected by very large angles, more than 90 degrees
- One out of about 12000 alpha particles appeared to rebound back, that is, retrace its path

- From the observations Rutherford concluded that:
- Most of the space inside the atom is empty because most of the alpha particles passed through the gold foil without getting deflected
- The positively charged part of the atom that repelled the positively charged alpha particles must be very small compared to the total size of the atom
- The positively charged particles must be occupying a very small volume in the atom, which is called the nucleus
- Since alpha particles are much heavier than electrons, they were not deflected by electrons

- Rutherford's nuclear model of the atom is like the solar system where the nucleus is the sun and the revolving electrons are the planets
- According to Rutherford's model, the mass of the atom is concentrated in the nucleus
- The nucleus is positively charged
- Electrons revolve around the nucleus in well-defined circular paths

- Drawbacks of Rutherford's model:
- It does not explain how electrons are distributed in different orbits around the nucleus
- The stability of the atom was not explained by this model
- According to classical electromagnetic theory, any accelerating charged particle must radiate electromagnetic radiation
- An electron revolving around the nucleus would continuously lose energy and eventually fall into the nucleus, making the atom unstable
- But in reality atoms are stable, which Rutherford's model could not explain

## 4.5 Bohr's Model of Atom

- Niels Bohr modified Rutherford's model of atom in 1913
- His model was based on the spectrum of the hydrogen atom

- Postulates of Bohr's model:
- The electrons in an atom revolve around the nucleus in certain definite circular paths called orbits or stationary states
- Each orbit or shell has a fixed energy, so these are also called energy levels
- The energy levels are numbered as 1, 2, 3, 4 or K, L, M, N starting from the nucleus
- The electron in a particular orbit has a constant energy, so these are called stationary states
- The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in a given shell is given by the formula 2n^2, where n is the orbit number
- For K shell n=1, so maximum electrons = 2(1)^2 = 2
- For L shell n=2, so maximum electrons = 2(2)^2 = 8
- For M shell n=3, so maximum electrons = 2(3)^2 = 18
- For N shell n=4, so maximum electrons = 2(4)^2 = 32

- Electrons revolve in these orbits without radiating energy, which explains the stability of atoms
- The energy of an electron in a given orbit is quantized, meaning it can have only certain fixed values

- Electrons can jump from one orbit to another:
- When an electron jumps from a lower energy level to a higher energy level, it absorbs energy
- When an electron jumps from a higher energy level to a lower energy level, it emits energy in the form of light or radiation
- This emission of energy explains the line spectra of hydrogen atom

- Valency is determined by the number of electrons in the outermost shell
- The outermost shell can have a maximum of 8 electrons
- If the outermost shell is completely filled with 8 electrons, the atom is chemically inert or unreactive
- If the outermost shell has less than 4 electrons, the atom tends to lose electrons
- If the outermost shell has more than 4 electrons, the atom tends to gain electrons

## 4.6 How are Electrons Distributed in Different Orbits or Shells?

- The distribution of electrons in different orbits is called electronic configuration
- The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in any shell is 2n^2 where n is the orbit number

- Rules for filling electrons:
- Electrons fill the innermost shell first before filling the outer shells
- The maximum number of electrons that can be present in the outermost shell is 8
- Electrons cannot be filled in a shell unless the inner shells are filled

- The electronic configuration of the first 20 elements:
- Hydrogen has 1 electron: K=1
- Helium has 2 electrons: K=2
- Lithium has 3 electrons: K=2, L=1
- Beryllium has 4 electrons: K=2, L=2
- Boron has 5 electrons: K=2, L=3
- Carbon has 6 electrons: K=2, L=4
- Nitrogen has 7 electrons: K=2, L=5
- Oxygen has 8 electrons: K=2, L=6
- Fluorine has 9 electrons: K=2, L=7
- Neon has 10 electrons: K=2, L=8
- Sodium has 11 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=1
- Magnesium has 12 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=2
- Aluminium has 13 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=3
- Silicon has 14 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=4
- Phosphorus has 15 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=5
- Sulphur has 16 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=6
- Chlorine has 17 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=7
- Argon has 18 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=8
- Potassium has 19 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=8, N=1
- Calcium has 20 electrons: K=2, L=8, M=8, N=2

## 4.7 Valency

- Valency is the combining capacity of an atom of an element
- It is determined by the number of electrons in the outermost shell

- If the outermost shell has 8 electrons, the atom is stable and has zero valency
- Atoms of noble gases like helium, neon, argon have completely filled outermost shells, so they have zero valency

- If the outermost shell has less than 4 electrons, the atom tends to lose those electrons to achieve a stable configuration
- For example sodium has configuration 2,8,1, so it loses 1 electron and its valency is 1
- For example magnesium has configuration 2,8,2, so it loses 2 electrons and its valency is 2

- If the outermost shell has more than 4 electrons, the atom tends to gain electrons to complete its octet
- For example chlorine has configuration 2,8,7, so it gains 1 electron and its valency is 1
- For example oxygen has configuration 2,6, so it gains 2 electrons and its valency is 2

- If the outermost shell has exactly 4 electrons, the atom tends to share electrons
- For example carbon has configuration 2,4, so it shares 4 electrons and its valency is 4

- The valency of an element is determined by the number of electrons in the outermost shell of its atom
- Elements with same number of valence electrons have similar chemical properties

## 4.8 Atomic Number and Mass Number

- Atomic number is defined as the number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom
- It is denoted by the letter Z
- The atomic number determines the identity of an element
- Two atoms of different elements cannot have the same atomic number
- In a neutral atom, the number of protons is equal to the number of electrons, so Z = number of protons = number of electrons

- Mass number is defined as the sum of the number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus of an atom
- It is denoted by the letter A
- Mass number = number of protons + number of neutrons
- Or A = Z + n, where n is the number of neutrons
- So the number of neutrons = Mass number - Atomic number, or n = A - Z

- The symbol used to represent an element is X with mass number A written as superscript and atomic number Z written as subscript: AZX

## 4.9 Isotopes and Isobars

- Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers
- Isotopes have the same number of protons but different number of neutrons
- Since isotopes have the same number of electrons, they have the same chemical properties
- However, they have different physical properties because of the difference in mass

- Examples of isotopes of hydrogen:
- Protium has 1 proton, 0 neutrons, mass number 1
- Deuterium has 1 proton, 1 neutron, mass number 2
- Tritium has 1 proton, 2 neutrons, mass number 3

- Examples of isotopes of carbon:
- Carbon-12 has 6 protons, 6 neutrons, mass number 12
- Carbon-13 has 6 protons, 7 neutrons, mass number 13
- Carbon-14 has 6 protons, 8 neutrons, mass number 14

- Isotopes of chlorine:
- Chlorine-35 has 17 protons, 18 neutrons, mass number 35
- Chlorine-37 has 17 protons, 20 neutrons, mass number 37

- Isobars are atoms of different elements having the same mass number but different atomic numbers
- Since isobars have different atomic numbers, they are different elements with different chemical properties
- They have different number of protons but the total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) is the same

- Examples of isobars:
- Argon has atomic number 18, mass number 40
- Potassium has atomic number 19, mass number 40
- Calcium has atomic number 20, mass number 40

- The atomicity of an element refers to the number of atoms present in one molecule of the element
- It is also called the number of atoms in a molecule
- For example oxygen is diatomic, meaning it has atomicity of 2, meaning one molecule of oxygen has 2 atoms
- Neon has atomicity of 1, meaning it exists as single atoms, it is monoatomic
